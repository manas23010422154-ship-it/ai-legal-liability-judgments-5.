## 2. Topic and Research Objective

Artificial Intelligence (AI) is increasingly being used for legal research, drafting and analysis. However, AI systems can generate incorrect information, including fabricated case citations and judgments that do not exist. When lawyers or courts rely on such information without verification, it can affect the fairness and reliability of judicial proceedings.

This project examines how the Supreme Court of India has addressed the use of AI-generated or allegedly AI-generated fabricated legal authorities. It focuses on human accountability, verification of legal sources and the responsibility of professionals who rely on AI-generated material. The project does not attempt to establish a comprehensive legal framework for every form of autonomous AI liability.

## 3. Research Methodology

The research focused on Supreme Court matters decided between 1 September 2021 and 31 August 2026. The research used published Supreme Court judgment and order texts, with search terms including "Artificial Intelligence," "AI-generated," "hallucinated," "fake judgments" and "synthetic judgments."

Cases were considered relevant when the judicial record directly addressed AI-generated or fabricated legal authorities used in judicial proceedings. The research examined the date, case number, procedural status, legal issue and judicial response. Particular care was taken to distinguish reasoned judgments from interim orders so that observations made during pending proceedings would not be incorrectly presented as final legal holdings.

The current dataset contains two identified matters: *Pooja Ramesh Singh v. Jammu and Kashmir Bank Ltd.* and *Gummadi Usha Rani v. Sure Mallikarjuna Rao*. This is a preliminary dataset, not proof that these are the only relevant Supreme Court matters. Further primary-source research is necessary to establish whether the dataset meets the assignment's target of 10–20 judgments.

## 5. Key Legal Observations

The research identifies human verification as a central safeguard in AI-assisted legal work. In *Pooja Ramesh Singh*, the Supreme Court addressed fabricated or misattributed authorities and emphasised the importance of authentic legal sources. *Gummadi Usha Rani* demonstrates judicial concern about allegedly non-existent AI-generated authorities and their use in court proceedings.

Together, these matters highlight the importance of checking citations, reading original judgments and maintaining human responsibility for legal submissions and judicial reasoning. However, they do not establish that AI systems possess independent legal personality or determine the general civil or criminal liability of autonomous AI systems.

## 6. Limitations

The dataset is limited to two identified matters and should be treated as preliminary. It cannot establish comprehensive trends across Indian courts or support general conclusions about all forms of AI liability. Further research and verification against official judicial records are necessary before drawing broader conclusions.
